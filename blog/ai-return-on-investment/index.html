80% of surveyed leaders at big US companies say AI pays off. 28% say it pays off big.

Here is a pair of numbers worth sitting with. In the 2026 AI Adoption Report from Wharton and GBK Collective, 80% of US enterprise leaders say their AI investments are already producing positive returns. But only 28% call those returns strongly positive. Four in five report getting something back; far fewer report a strong return. So the useful question is no longer "does AI pay off" but "what do the companies reporting strong returns actually look like" - and this year the study answers that in unusual detail. [1]

The study

This is the fourth annual study from Wharton Human-AI Research and GBK Collective, published October 13, 2026. About 1,400 US respondents took the 15-minute online survey between July 9 and July 29, 2026. Comparisons with earlier years use the managers-and-above base (n=1,007), and for the first time the study also polled entry- and mid-level employees, so it can compare what leaders report with what their teams experience. [1][2]

What the strong-return group has in common

The most common way departments judge AI is hours saved - 40% use it, while net-new revenue is the least-used major measure at 27%. Among departments that also track at least one growth measure (net-new revenue, sales conversion, output volume or customer satisfaction), 35% report strongly positive returns. Among those measuring efficiency alone, 13% do. [2]

Settled approaches show up in the strong group too. Where a company has newly appointed a Chief AI Officer, 58% report strongly positive returns, against 24% where AI was added to an existing executive's role and 8% with no such role or plans. Where the company has standardized on one approved AI platform, 50% report strongly positive returns, against 9% of those still experimenting without a defined approach. [1]

The strongest pattern is workflow change. The report defines transformation as AI fully embedded in core processes, agents in use, and workflows significantly changed; only 20% of departments have attained all three of those transformation milestones, but 67% of those that have report strongly positive returns, against 19% of the rest. Departments with strong returns are also more likely to have changed their management structure (64% vs 47%), to monitor AI use (92% vs 74%), to include AI in performance reviews (87% vs 65%) and to report adequately trained employees (91% vs 73%). [1][2]

The awkward part about AI agents

Two-thirds of departments say they use AI agents, but only 17% let an agent complete a task without a person approving each step. GBK's Brian Smith calls this the supervised middle: "Most companies are sitting in a supervised middle, paying for autonomy and then reviewing every action, and that is where returns are weakest." The report adds two related findings: where agents may take irreversible financial or legal actions, 29% of departments have transformed their workflows against 13% of those allowing agents more limited responsibilities, and departments that monitor agents automatically have transformed at 24% against 9%. [1]

Read that carefully, because it is easy to over-read. These are associations in self-reported data: this survey cannot say that approving each step is what holds returns back, and it would be a mistake to conclude that oversight is the problem. The pattern the report itself points to is not "remove the human" but consequential work paired with automated monitoring - oversight by exception rather than by keystroke. Where the boundary should sit depends on what a mistake would cost, and no survey can decide that for your business.

What leaders say vs what employees say

For the first time, the study polled entry- and mid-level employees alongside leaders, and the two respondent groups give noticeably different pictures. Leaders report organizational alignment - adequate training, a clear long-term vision, real choice in how people use AI - at 39%; only 26% of entry- and mid-level employees say the same. 78% of leaders say employees receive adequate AI training; 56% of employees agree. 79% of leaders say people are confident using AI daily, against 62% of employees; on leadership setting out a clear long-term AI vision, it is 79% against 64%. These are separate respondent groups, not necessarily people at the same companies - so the numbers show how differently the two levels tend to see things, not a proven mismatch inside any one firm. [2]

And something shifted in mood this year. Daily use among leaders jumped 16 points to 62%, yet "excited" fell 8 points to 51% while "cautious" rose 7 to 44%, "skeptical" 8 to 25% and "worried" 5 to 16%. It is the first year in the study where usage and enthusiasm move in opposite directions. Employees, for their part, are not anti-AI: 73% of entry- and mid-level employees say it has had a positive effect on their own role, and 84% of those who use it say it saves them time. [2]

Fair warnings

  • This is one self-reported online survey of US enterprises, run in July 2026 with about 1,400 respondents. It polled leaders and employees at large companies - not small businesses. A ten-person firm is not in this data, and the returns reported here are what respondents say, not audited figures. [2]
  • Every comparison in this post is an association. Companies already doing well may find it easier to reorganize, name an AI owner or settle on one platform - the survey cannot say which came first. The report's own word is "correlates", and its authors frame these as markers, not recipes. [1]
  • The study is co-produced by GBK Collective, a commercial research firm whose business includes AI adoption research; Wharton directs the research agenda and publishes the report. Worth knowing when a study about AI returns also recommends deeper integration. [1]

What to do with this

What follows is our read for a small business - judgment, not the study's prescription. It surveyed enterprises, the findings are associations, and none of it is a guarantee.

  1. Measure one outcome that isn't hours. Departments tracking a growth measure report strongly positive returns at 35% against 13% for efficiency-only tracking. For a small business the translation is simple: pick one number the tool should move - sales, repeat customers, quotes sent - and check it monthly. [2]
  2. Name one owner. You do not need a Chief AI Officer; you need one named person, usually you, who decides which tools stay and which go. The pattern in the strong-return group is a settled approach, not a specific job title. [1]
  3. Match the review to the risk. Do not read this survey as proof that approvals hurt returns - it cannot show that. Our read: for reversible, low-stakes tasks, reviewing the finished work afterwards may be enough; for anything touching money, contracts or customers, keep a person approving each step. The report's own framing points the same way: consequential work, monitored automatically - not unmonitored.
  4. Ask, don't assume, on training and direction. In this survey, 78% of leaders say employees receive adequate AI training and 56% of employees agree - a gap between the two respondent groups, not proof about any one company. The only way to know your own team's answer is to ask: do you feel trained on this, and do you know what we are trying to achieve with it? [2]

Try it this week

Take your biggest AI expense and write one sentence: "By the end of this quarter, this should move [one number that isn't hours]." Then ask the person who uses it most the two questions above. If the answers surprise you, this survey says you would be in good company.
